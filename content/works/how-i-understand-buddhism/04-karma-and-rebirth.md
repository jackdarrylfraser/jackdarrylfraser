## Karma & Rebirth

This brings us to the ideas through which many people first encounter Buddhism: karma as more than ordinary cause and effect, and rebirth after death. These ideas can seem at odds with both common Judeo-Christian beliefs and secular humanism. I have tried to approach them by reconsidering assumptions I had taken for granted, while keeping in mind how much I still do not know.

When I paid attention to my experience, I found a kind of moment-to-moment continuity that helped me understand the language of rebirth. Many of my thoughts seemed to arise without my choosing them. When I tried to find the thought that felt most like “me,” I was often drawn to the most emotional ones. Often before I could even recognize a thought was happening, I was already attached to it; carrying it out in day to day life or making plans to. It was impossible to discern that I had not authored it.

Following that observation led me to question how much control I have over my emotions. Many arise in response to conditions I did not choose. This made me feel like I had no agency, and it made a fixed, independent self harder to locate. Even the thoughts that feel most convincingly like “me” seem to depend on causes and conditions that were verifiably not me.

The image of a person as a wave in the ocean helped me make sense of this. The water molecules change, but the wave continues as a recognizable pattern. In the same way, the person can be understood as a continuity through change rather than as an unchanging essence. The wave eventually breaks on the shore, which also makes the image a moving metaphor for death, decomposition, and the return of our bodies to the wider processes of life.

This account fit my secular understanding of Buddhism, but I wondered whether I was too quickly setting aside buddhism's traditional teachings. Perhaps I had misunderstood karma and rebirth, or assumed too confidently that they had to be literal claims about something supernatural and would try to hand wave away any attempt as a real explanation. The teaching of non-self had already challenged some of my most basic assumptions; perhaps these teachings called for the same openness.

The distinction between conventional and ultimate truth helped me think about this. As I understand it, karma and rebirth can function as conventional teachings: ways of describing continuity and orienting how we live, rather than final statements about an independent, permanent reality. But if they are conventional, what makes them worth taking seriously as guides for life? It is fair to ask whether a more ultimate account would be more compelling.

Here I find dependent origination useful. In my current understanding, *nāma-rūpa* (“name and form”) points to the way experience and the world we describe arise through conditions. We encounter things through perception, concepts, and relationships; those encounters shape how we understand them. Which makes it clear that what I can know about the world is inseparable from the conditions through which I experience it.

Consider a rock and a tree. I can describe their colors, shapes, textures, and smells, but each description depends on a particular capacity to perceive. If I could no longer see, I would lose access to their color and shape; if I could no longer touch them, I would lose another kind of information. The objects might still exist, but my account of them would become more and more limited. Eventually once I no longer retain any ability to perceive them my lack of perception would be, from my perspective, the same as them not existing. This is an epistemic limit: it tells me what I can experience and verify, not by itself what exists beyond that experience.

The problem of other minds makes this limit especially vivid. I cannot directly experience another person's point of view, yet I have good reason to treat them as a person with an inner life. Thought experiments about beings that behave like people but have no inner experience can make the question difficult, but they do not settle it. They show how much of what I believe about other minds rests on inference rather than direct access.

The same follows for me about rebirth. I cannot directly expeirence what will happen to me in my next life, yet I feel I have good reason to treat rebirth as the working hypothesis through which I measure the value of my actions. 

Everything I have learned about the brain points to how closely our inner life depends on bodily conditions. At the same time, describing physical processes from the outside is distinct from explaining what it feels like to inhabit them from within. This leaves me with an open question: when the body ceases to function, does conscious experience simply terminate, or does the chain of causes and conditions sustain some form of continuity?

I do not treat the explanatory gap between matter and consciousness as evidence for rebirth. But recognizing that our current physical models are incomplete leaves room for an open mind. For me, accepting other minds and remaining open to rebirth share a common thread: both require navigating an inferential gap where direct first-person access is impossible. While the evidence for other minds is far more immediate, the comparison is a reminder that unmediated empirical proof is not the only ground for adopting a meaningful framework for living.

These limits do not, by themselves, provide evidence for rebirth or mean that science is mistaken. They remind me that third-person scientific methods can study the conditions associated with consciousness without directly giving access to first-person experience. I want to remain aligned with scientific inquiry while also honoring the limitations of science itself as a method of inquiry. 

Science can describe many of the conditions that shape experience, but it does not, by itself, tell me what to value or how I ought to act. For that, I need an ethical framework that takes my experience and relationships with others seriously. I find karma useful for reflecting on how intentions and actions shape experience and affect others; rebirth extends that perspective beyond this life. The householder precepts give me practical guidance for acting with care, cultivating meaning, and contributing to my community. I use these teachings as working models, not as claims whose usefulness proves their metaphysical truth.

If someone asks whether I literally believe I will be reborn in another life, I would say yes, but nobody knows what happens after you die. By that, I mean I treat rebirth as a guiding belief and try to let it shape my actions toward compassion and wisdom—not that I can prove what happens after death.

By rebirth, I do not mean reincarnation as an unchanged self moving from one body to another. I mean the continuation of causes and conditions in a mind-stream, without a permanent self passing through it. In this sense, the person I am at sixty is not exactly the same as the child I was at three, but there is a continuity shaped by what came before.
